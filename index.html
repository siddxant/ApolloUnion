Three problems we are solving

01
Flagship

Silent diseases that medicine misses in Indians

Western risk models (Framingham, QRISK) are miscalibrated for South Asians. Indians develop diabetes at lower BMI, heart disease a decade earlier, and CKD largely undetected. Apollo's longitudinal lab data can predict these conditions years before diagnosis — from tests people already get.

CKD is the sharpest wedge. Creatinine and eGFR trends in longitudinal data show kidney function declining years before any clinical symptom. India carries an enormous CKD burden — most undetected, most presenting in late stages when intervention changes little. Early prediction from routine bloodwork changes that.

The same modelling framework extends to type 2 diabetes onset at sub-diagnostic HbA1c levels, and fatty liver disease identified years before elevated enzymes trigger a clinical referral.

02
Pharma Research

Drugs approved on Western trials, prescribed to 140 Cr Indians

Pivotal clinical trials rarely include Indian patients. Drug effectiveness and safety signals in Indian populations remain largely unknown — until a medicine has already been prescribed at scale. Real-world evidence from 1.1 Cr patient records closes that gap.

Indian patients differ from trial populations in genetics, diet, comorbidity patterns, and drug metabolism — differences that affect both efficacy and adverse event rates. Yet pharmaceutical companies enter the Indian market using trial data from cohorts that do not represent Indian patients.

Apollo Union generates population-specific evidence from actual prescribing and outcome records: which drugs perform differently in Indian patients, where safety signals emerge, and what dosing patterns produce the best results. This is data pharmaceutical companies require for post-marketing surveillance, regulatory submissions to CDSCO, and commercial decisions in the world's most populous drug market.

03
Pharmacogenomics

Drugs that don't work on South Asians — or cause harm

Clopidogrel is among the most widely prescribed medicines for stroke and heart attack prevention. For roughly 1 in 3 Indians carrying CYP2C19 loss-of-function variants, it provides no antiplatelet protection. In some cases, it has caused death. The problem is not the drug. It is that the drug was never studied in this population.

Clopidogrel is a prodrug activated by the liver enzyme CYP2C19. Loss-of-function alleles (*2, *3) are carried by approximately 25–30% of South Asians — a significantly higher rate than European populations. In patients with these variants, clopidogrel produces inadequate platelet inhibition, making prescribed therapy effectively useless, while the patient and physician believe they are protected.

This is one documented case among many. South Asian populations have distinct pharmacogenomic profiles affecting drug metabolism, receptor sensitivity, and adverse reaction rates across multiple drug classes. Identifying which medicines behave differently in Indian patients — and why — requires Indian patient data at scale. That is precisely what this dataset enables.

Apollo Union's prescription and outcomes data, matched with diagnostic trends, can surface pharmacogenomic signals across the Indian population and provide the evidence base for updated prescribing guidelines and drug development tailored to South Asian patients.
